# Tammy editorial voice: the patient accountant

**Who it sounds like:** a financially literate human explaining a real decision over a desk, with a pencil and the source documents open. Intelligent, careful, approachable. No breathless SaaS marketing. No corporate reassurance filler.

## Four moves to make every article distinct

1. Start from an everyday mistake, dilemma or document. Example: "The invoice is due, but the money has not landed."
2. Show *why* with the correct technical distinction. Use one proper term, then interpret it.
3. Prove it with a tiny concrete model: assumptions, line items, totals and a boundary. Every number should be reproducible.
4. Return to a decision the reader can make: which figure to check, which record to keep, what a practical next step is.

## Editing pass

- Replace "It is important to note that" with the fact.
- Replace "Businesses can benefit from" with a specific action and consequence.
- Replace "Understanding X is crucial" with the moment when X matters.
- Replace empty superlatives and vague AI claims with the exact function of a tool.
- Vary sentence length and structure. No robotic three-part paragraph rhythm. One excellent analogy is better than five generic ones.
- Keep the narrative human but never invent a personal experience, named accountant or customer quote.
- Lead with the uncomfortable distinction when it is true. An invoice is not cash. An ABN that passes a checksum is not proof of registration.
- Australian spelling: organise, recognised, licence (noun), GST, BAS, ATO, ABR. Do not assume all businesses have the same GST treatment.
- Keep sections connected; every heading should advance the argument. Avoid generic "Key takeaways", "Conclusion", "Unlock your potential" and fake "FAQ" search pages unless the question itself genuinely needs one.
- Prefer one precise example over a page of bullet lists. Show what changes if the assumptions change.

## Fact/claim checks

- GST values: identify whether they are inclusive or exclusive, the tax treatment of each line, and rounding basis.
- Profit/cash: state whether example is accrual or cash, whether amounts include GST, and what movements are excluded.
- Receivables ageing: label the as-of date; overdue buckets start *after* the due date; define whether values are aged by invoice or due date.
- Break-even: fixed vs variable costs, unit contribution and ceil-to-whole-unit rounding; if contribution <= 0, increasing volume alone cannot cover positive fixed costs.
- Charts: caption says `illustrative` unless data is a published, sourced measurement; never fake market trends or tax statistics.
- Source at the place of claim, plus one minimal source panel where it improves confidence. No footnote pile that replaces clarity.

## Mini example (style, not copy)

Weak: "In today's dynamic business landscape, managing outstanding invoices is more important than ever."

Better: "The invoice may be only ten days old, but it is already three days overdue. Those are different clocks. An ageing report that starts counting from issue date will tell the wrong story."

## Further human-writing checks

- A real example should have a direction: if cash ledger is lower than the bank, investigate a duplicate outgoing posting or a missing receipt, not a missing bank charge.
- "Cash" and "accrual" can refer to GST attribution, income tax or financial accounting. Identify which system you mean before comparing dates.
- A bank credit is evidence of a receipt, not proof of sales. Match it to a source transaction before naming the type.
- When a block has alternate states, explain what changes and what **does not** change (for example, GST timing versus total GST).
- Do not congratulate readers for ticking a checklist. The tool records no external action or evidence.
