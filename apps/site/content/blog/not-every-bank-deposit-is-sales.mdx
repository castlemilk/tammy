Three deposits arrive in a business bank account on the same day. The bank feed displays a pleasing total: **$8,700 received**.

That is money in the account. It is not necessarily $8,700 of sales.

The distinction matters when you reconcile statements, calculate turnover, or explain cash movements to someone preparing the books. A bank statement tells you **what moved**; the supporting evidence tells you **why**.

<h2 id="one-day-three-reasons">Three receipts, three different explanations</h2>

Imagine a GST-registered Australian sole trader. For this simplified example, all three transactions happen on the same day, the sale is a new cash sale that has not already been posted, and the supply is fully taxable at the standard 10% GST rate.

- A customer pays **$2,200** for a sale, including $200 GST.
- A lender advances **$5,000** under an ordinary business loan agreement.
- The owner transfers **$1,500** from personal savings into the business bank account.

The total received is $2,200 + $5,000 + $1,500 = **$8,700**.

<h2 id="classify-before-you-report">Classify a receipt before calling it revenue</h2>

Under these assumptions, only **$2,000** is sales revenue excluding GST. A further **$200** is GST collected on the taxable sale. The loan creates an obligation to repay the lender. The owner's transfer represents money contributed to the business, rather than payment by a customer.

This is an accounting classification example, not a complete statement of the tax treatment of every loan or owner transaction. Entity type matters: shareholder contributions to a company, for instance, need their own documents and appropriate treatment.

<Callout tone="caution" title="Do not record the same sale twice">

If the customer is paying an invoice that has already been recognised in receivables, recording revenue again when the money lands would double-count the sale. The usual receipt entry would reduce the existing receivable instead. Trace the payment to the original document first.

</Callout>

<h2 id="bank-statement-is-evidence">The statement is evidence, not the full explanation</h2>

For each receipt, retain enough detail to identify the reason it arrived. A customer payment might link to an invoice or till record. A loan deposit should link to the agreement and liability. An owner's transfer may need a clear reference to the originating account and purpose.

The ATO's [bank-account record-keeping guidance](https://smallbusiness.taxsuperandyou.gov.au/record-keeping/bank-accounts) specifically lists records of deposits, bank statements and loan or lease agreements. It also encourages sole traders to keep business and personal transactions separate so the records can be explained more easily.

<h2 id="reconcile-the-reason">Reconcile the reason, not just the amount</h2>

Start with the statement, match the date and amount, then open the supporting evidence. Decide whether the receipt is a sale, settlement of an existing receivable, financing, capital, a transfer between your own accounts, a refund or something else. Avoid assigning all unexplained money to sales just because the deposits reconcile numerically.
