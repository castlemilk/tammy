A business can show $8,000 of profit in its accounts and still add only $5,000 to its bank balance over the same period. The missing $3,000 isn't automatically an error or a vanished expense. It may simply be a matter of timing.

To make sense of the difference, you need to know what the profit figure measures, what cash actually moved, and which balances changed in between.

<h2 id="two-measures">Profit and cash answer different questions</h2>

Under **accrual accounting**, income is generally recorded when earned and expenses when incurred, subject to the applicable recognition rules. Cash flow describes amounts that actually enter and leave the bank or cash accounts. A customer who owes you money can contribute to revenue without having paid. A supplier invoice can create an expense before you settle the bill.

That difference isn't a reason to prefer one report universally. Profit helps explain performance over a period. Cash flow tells you whether there is money available to meet obligations when they fall due. A business needs both views.

<Callout tone="note" title="A good profit figure cannot pay a bill by itself">

Sales may be recognised before cash is collected. The bank statement records settlement, not the whole economic activity of the month.

</Callout>

<h2 id="the-month-on-paper">One month, two outcomes</h2>

Consider a fictional services business. All figures below are **excluding GST**, and the simplified model excludes opening unpaid balances, stock movements, financing, asset purchases, tax and other adjustments.

- The business invoices $12,000 of work completed during the month.
- Customers pay $8,000 of that amount before month-end.
- The business incurs $4,000 of operating expenses.
- It pays $3,000 of those expenses before month-end.

On that basis, accrual profit from the illustrated activity is $12,000 minus $4,000, or **$8,000**. Net cash movement from that same activity is $8,000 received minus $3,000 paid, or **$5,000**.

<WorkedExample title="Where did the $3,000 difference come from?">

The $4,000 of invoiced work not yet collected increases receivables. The $1,000 of incurred costs not yet paid increases payables. The simplified difference is **$4,000 receivables minus $1,000 unpaid costs = $3,000**. That reconciles the $8,000 profit and $5,000 net cash movement for this specific example.

</WorkedExample>

<h2 id="the-bridge">Build a bridge, not an excuse</h2>

<Formula label="Simplified profit-to-cash bridge" expression="Profit − increase in receivables + increase in payables" result="$8,000 − $4,000 + $1,000 = $5,000" />

The bridge above is deliberately narrow. A real cash-flow reconciliation can also require changes in inventory, prepaid expenses, accrued liabilities, loans, tax, capital expenditure, depreciation and other non-cash items. It would be wrong to force all differences into receivables and payables just because this example works that way.

This is also why a bank-statement reconciliation and a profit-and-loss review are not substitutes for one another. The statement verifies bank movements; the ledger explains their classification and the broader position.

<h2 id="plan-from-both">Plan from both reports</h2>

For routine decisions, review sales, direct costs, unpaid customer invoices, bills coming due and expected bank receipts together. When projecting whether cash will cover payroll or tax, use expected payment dates rather than recognised profit. When deciding if prices need to change, inspect margins and costs rather than bank balance alone.

The Australian Government's [cash-flow statement guide](https://business.gov.au/finance/cash-flow/set-up-a-cash-flow-statement) recommends separating opening balances, incoming cash, outgoing payments and closing balances. It also asks that forecasts make clear whether amounts include or exclude GST.
